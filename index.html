About

I am an applied economist working on labour markets, education and productivity.

My research examines how access to labour, firm investment and labour-market policy shape economic outcomes. I work with company accounts, labour force surveys and administrative records, using applied microeconometric methods to answer policy-relevant questions.

I am completing a PhD in Economics at the University of Glasgow. My thesis, Three Essays on Productivity and Investment in the UK, was submitted in September 2026, with the viva scheduled for November 2026.

Research

Work in progress

Employer-Sponsored Migration as a Firm Capability: Evidence on Firm Productivity

With Dimitris Christelis

The causal productivity effects of holding a licence that enables UK firms to recruit workers through sponsored visas.

Research summary

This study links UK company accounts to government records of employers authorised to sponsor migrant workers. It uses partial identification to bound the causal effects of holding a sponsorship licence under the UK’s pre-2020 rules. Under the identifying assumptions, it finds positive effects on sponsors’ labour and total factor productivity.

Work in progress

Investment and Firm Productivity: Partial Identification Evidence from UK Firms

With Dimitris Christelis and John Tsoukalas

The causal effects of tangible-investment intensity on UK firms’ productivity.

Research summary

This study constructs investment, capital-stock and productivity measures from UK company accounts. It uses partial identification to bound the causal effects of tangible-investment intensity. Under the identifying assumptions, medium and high investment raise firms’ labour and total factor productivity relative to low investment.

Work in progress

Minimum-Wage Exposure and Investment Composition: Evidence from UK Sectors

Sole-authored research

How investment responses vary with sectors’ exposure to minimum-wage increases.

Research summary

This study combines the UK Labour Force Survey with sectoral data on employment, investment and capital stocks. It uses exposure-based difference-in-differences with sector-specific trends and distributed lags. The results suggest higher machinery investment in more-exposed sectors, but provide no robust evidence of broader investment or employment responses.

Working paper

Schooling and Labor Market Effects of the Extension of Non-Compulsory High School Education: Quasi-experimental Evidence from Turkey

With Meltem Dayıoğlu and Sezgin Polat

The schooling and labour-market effects of extending non-compulsory high school education in Turkey.

Working paper (ResearchGate) ↗

Research summary

We examine the causal effect of an education reform that increased the required years of schooling to obtain a high school diploma by one year on the educational and employment outcomes of teenagers and young adults in a context where high school attendance is non-compulsory. Using a regression discontinuity design, we find that the reform increases school enrolment, primarily by reducing the NEET probability of teenagers. However, adult men’s probability of high school graduation decreases as a result of the reform while no such adverse effect is observed for women. Furthermore, spill-over effects are observed in women’s post-secondary education attainment. While the reform effect on adult men is limited to a slight reduction in their employment probability, it has favourable effects on women in terms of wages earned, hours worked, and the quality of jobs held. Given that the reform does not change the wage employment probability of women, we interpret these results as stemming from the human capital effects of the reform.

JEL codes: I24, I25, I26, J24

Keywords: Non-compulsory Education, Natural Experiment, Schooling, Employment, Youth, Gender, Turkey
